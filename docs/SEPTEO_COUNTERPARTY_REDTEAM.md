# Reading the draft from STP's side
## What Septeo will object to, what they will ask, and what each concession costs you

Companion to the consistency review. Same draft of 14 September 2026.

**The shape of it.** Read from Karlsruhe, this document gives STP the whole client-facing risk and almost none of the protection. STP contracts in its own name with notaries, law firms and regulated institutions; it owes them first-level support, it passes through terms it cannot verify, its own liability to you is uncapped, and yours to it is capped at a number that can approach zero. Meanwhile it must hand you its end-client pricing case by case while you stay free to sell to the same clients. Three objections will do most of the work in the first call. Everything else is detail around them.

---

## 1. The three that can stall the deal

### 1.1 You see their price book and stay free to compete
Clause 8.5 requires STP to report, per Case, the amount it invoiced its client, with an annual reconciliation against its billing records. Clause 3.6 keeps you free to sell directly "including to clients of the kind STP serves". Clause 8.1 leaves STP to set prices it must then disclose to you.

Put together: a supplier with a direct channel into the same segment gets a case-level view of the reseller's margin. Their commercial lead will see this in the first read, and it is the objection with the most force behind it, because it is true. Clause 6.2 does not answer it — 6.2 only restrains you as to End Clients "disclosed to it through Cases", and under 8.5 no names are disclosed, so 6.2 protects nothing while 6.1 binds them for 24 months against your entire supply chain. The asymmetry is visible on the page.

*What they will ask for:* a fixed purchase price instead of a share; or aggregated reporting through an auditor; or exclusivity in the legal/notarial/tax segment; or a standstill on your direct sales to their named clients.

*What it costs you:* the auditor route is cheap and it also repairs your § 89b position (identities stop flowing to you altogether). Segment exclusivity is expensive and cuts across your DATEV channel — that is your real chip, spend it deliberately. Moving to a purchase price you may want anyway for the characterisation question.

### 1.2 The liability construction is not sellable to their legal team
13.2 caps you at your share under 8.2 in the preceding twelve months — plausibly €18.000, less in year one, and the 8.4 minimum is not even in the base. STP's own liability is unregulated and therefore statutory and unlimited. There is no indemnity in either direction and, critically, **no IP indemnity and no warranty that you hold the rights you purport to grant**. Clause 3.5 says the rights "follow what Founders1 holds in the underlying content" — you are telling them in the operative text that the scope is undefined and may be constrained by an upstream agreement they cannot see.

They carry the end client, the regulated end client, and the register's own restrictions, with no recourse. Expect their counsel to treat this as the deal point.

*What they will ask for:* a mutual cap at a real figure (not a fee multiple — the fees are too small relative to the exposure), carve-outs for data protection and IP, an IP/title indemnity, and evidence of professional indemnity insurance.

*What it costs you:* the title warranty is uncomfortable while the upstream agreement is unresolved — which is exactly why they will want it. A capped IP indemnity with your own carve-out for register-imposed restrictions notified under Annex 5 (e) is the defensible middle. Raising the cap to a fixed sum is cheaper than it looks and buys goodwill on 12.2, the clause you actually care about.

### 1.3 Take-or-pay on an unproven product
€1.500 per month from month three, 24 months firm, with:
- no volume or coverage commitment from you in return;
- no service credits (7.3 is a target with no remedy) and no relief from the minimum in a month the API fails;
- no pro-rata reduction when jurisdictions fall away under 14.2a below the 50 % threshold;
- no exit if the product simply does not sell;
- uncapped price changes after twelve months under 8.6.

*What they will ask for:* a ramp, a 12-month initial term or termination for convenience after 12 months, reduction of the minimum where availability or coverage fails, and a cap on price increases.

*What it costs you:* the availability and coverage linkages are cheap and fair, and you should offer them unprompted — they cost nothing if the service works. Shortening the term is expensive. Capping the general price-change route (8.6 first limb) to an index, and leaving the register-fee route as the only uncapped one, is a good trade.

## 2. What their compliance and DPO functions will flag

**DORA, clauses 9.5 and 9.6.** 9.5 tells STP it must meet its regulated clients' ICT third-party requirements itself, and simultaneously forbids it from committing you to audit rights, route disclosure, exit assistance, incident reporting or register-of-information entries. Those are precisely what its clients will require. As drafted the clause asks STP to promise something and then removes the means. Their bank-facing clients will not accept "the supplier may charge its reasonable cost" for incident cooperation — Art. 30 (3) (f) DORA expects assistance at no cost or at a **pre-determined** cost — nor a flat refusal to disclose the procurement chain, which Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/532 pushes down to you as STP's subcontractor.
*Cheap to give:* a pre-priced cooperation schedule, an agreed incident-notification window, your LEI and register-of-information data, and route disclosure to an independent auditor under NDA rather than to the client. Offering this yourself converts a fight into a selling point for their sales team.

**The controller/processor question.** Their DPO template will ask for an AVV, and their end clients will ask for one down the chain. Annex 3 is the right instrument on the facts, but you will have to argue it, and Annex 3 currently omits the fact that most supports it — that you hold and re-serve documents to other customers (Annex 1.4 § 4). Add it.

**Subprocessors.** Annex 3.4 names hosting and sub-processors "on request", with notification before a change but no list, no objection right and no published register. Regulated end clients require all three. Related: your published subprocessor list includes an LLM provider for enrichment and classification. If any part of the registry service touches it, they will ask, and it is better answered in Annex 3 than in a due-diligence questionnaire six weeks in.

**Third-country transfers.** Annex 3.4 covers storage in the EU/EEA. Procurement in 153 jurisdictions sends identifiers outward — Russia, Belarus, Iran, UAE, the offshore registers. Nothing in Annex 3 addresses Art. 44–49. Their DPO will find it, and Russia, Belarus and Iran sitting in the covered list will separately reach a French group's sanctions function.

**Rights scope.** 3.5 and Annex 5 (b) leave the licence scope open ("the applicable scope is stated with each Document"), and Annex 5.3 lets you amend the pass-through terms unilaterally on 30 days' notice. STP cannot tell a notary in advance whether a document may stay in the client file for ten years. They will want a defined minimum scope that cannot be reduced, and a lead time long enough to re-paper their general terms.

## 3. What the group will object to

**Clause 5.2** bans building, operating or supplying a competing registry, coverage or procurement product, with no time limit, surviving termination indefinitely under 14.3, and drafted broadly enough to catch a Septeo subsidiary that never touched your data. For a group of Septeo's size this is uncontrollable and their corporate counsel will not sign it as written.
*Cheap to give:* limit it to use of information actually obtained from you, add a residual-knowledge carve-out, scope it to the entities that receive the data, and give it the same 24-month horizon as 6.1. You lose little; the real protection is 6.1 and the database right.

**Clause 6.1** bans approaching or contracting with your registers and suppliers for 24 months post-term, with no carve-out for relationships STP already has or identifies independently. An information business will have some. Expect a pre-existing-relationships carve-out to be non-negotiable for them — and note that an obligation not to approach *official public registers* is not defensible anyway.

**Clause 4.** Annex 4 is empty at signature, accession needs your confirmation, STP is liable for every affiliate as for itself, and an entity leaving the group loses its rights immediately — which breaks a divestment mid-flight. They will want a pre-populated list, deemed approval after a deadline, and a wind-down for departing entities.

## 4. The questions their sales and pricing team will ask

These are not legal objections, but they arrive in the same call and they are the ones that make people doubt the product.

- **"What do we tell clients about turnaround?"** 9.3 forbids statements beyond Annex 1; Annex 1 has no turnaround figures; 7.2 points to Annex 2 and Annex 2.4 points to Annex 2a, and neither contains any. The honest answer today is "nothing", which is not a workable sales position.
- **"What do we tell them about coverage?"** 153 jurisdictions, no commitment that any given register works, and jurisdictions may fall away under 14.2a. They will want the fifteen priority jurisdictions committed for the term — cheap for you to give on the ones you own-source, worth asking your supplier about for the rest.
- **"Why do we pay full tier price for a data-only answer?"** Annex 1.3 prices data-only at the full base price, and the price follows the jurisdiction, not the document count. A client paying €15 for structured particulars and no document will complain to STP, not to you.
- **"And for a Case that returns nothing?"** Annex 1.3 says no charge; clause 8.7 says the procurement cost is payable. Fix before they ask (see A2 in the consistency review) — being caught on it costs more than the money.
- **"Can we order a certified extract specifically?"** No. Annex 1.2 delivers the highest obtainable level. A client who needs a sealed document cannot order one and cannot be told in advance whether it will get one.
- **"Can our law-firm clients use this for transaction due diligence?"** As drafted, no. Annex 5 (a) excludes discharging another person's compliance obligations, while 3.4 names legal practices as the target market. That contradiction is a commercial decision you should make before they raise it.
- **"United States and Brazil?"** They will ask which sub-registers, and the US is likely to be a priority jurisdiction. Your own brief records that you do not yet know.

## 5. One thing they may not negotiate — and should worry you more than the ones they do

Their counsel can simply let the document be AGB. It is your draft, it is a template, clause 3.6 contemplates other resellers, and § 305 ff. BGB then measure it. On that reading they do not need to win 13.2, clause 5 or 12.2 at the table; they take them as invalid later. A counterparty that accepts your restrictive clauses with little resistance is not necessarily agreeing with them.

The practical consequence is counter-intuitive: **you want visible negotiation on 5, 6.1, 12.2 and 13.2 specifically**, with a record of what you offered to change and what you conceded, clause by clause. Concessions on those four are worth more as evidence of *Aushandeln* than they cost as substance.

## 6. Where you are actually strong

Worth knowing so you do not over-concede.

- No contractual penalty anywhere — good for them, they will not raise it.
- 3.5 gives end clients retention beyond termination, which is what a notary needs and what most data suppliers refuse.
- 8.7 charges nothing for Cases you decline or that fail on your side; 12.3 re-procures free.
- 14.2a lets them walk away from a failed jurisdiction at no cost.
- 15.2 lets them assign to a successor without your consent — a gift to an acquisitive group, and one you may want to take back (see B5 in the consistency review) before they notice they have it.
- STP's liability being uncapped is a drafting accident in your favour. Expect to lose it; price the loss.
